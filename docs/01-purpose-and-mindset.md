# 01. Purpose and mindset

## What a proposal is

A research proposal is an argument, not a report. It argues that:

1. a specific gap in knowledge exists and matters;
2. a specific question, if answered, would close or narrow that gap;
3. a specific method can answer that question;
4. you can execute that method within the time, funding and infrastructure available.

Everything in the document serves one of those four claims. Anything that does not serve them is padding.

## What a proposal is not

- **Not a literature review.** The literature review is in service of the gap. If a paragraph does not help establish the gap or justify the method, cut it.
- **Not a results chapter.** You are not expected to have answers. You are expected to have a plan that could produce them.
- **Not a contract carved in stone.** The UP Library guide puts it plainly: the proposal is a map, and you will have room to manoeuvre. Scope will shift. The *question* should not.
- **Not a test of how much you can write.** Committees read many proposals. Density of argument beats volume of text.

## The Heilmeier catechism

George Heilmeier, as director of DARPA, required every proposed programme to answer a short set of questions. They remain the fastest sanity check available. Answer each in two sentences, in plain language, before you write anything else:

1. What are you trying to do? No jargon.
2. How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?
3. What is new in your approach, and why do you think it will succeed?
4. Who cares? If you succeed, what difference will it make?
5. What are the risks?
6. How much will it cost (time, compute, equipment)?
7. How long will it take?
8. What are the midterm and final checks for success?

If you cannot answer one of these, that is your next conversation with your supervisor, not a gap to paper over with prose.

## The viva test, applied early

The CERG-FLUX Lab (Fluids, Learning and Uncertainty in compleX systems) uses one test for everything a student produces: *could you defend this, alone, without notes, to a sceptical expert?*

Apply it to every sentence. If you have written "the Lattice Boltzmann Method is well suited to multiphase flows" and you cannot say *why* in three sentences without looking anything up, you do not yet own that claim. Either learn it until you do, or remove it.

## Who reads your proposal

| Reader | What they want | Consequence if absent |
|---|---|---|
| Your supervisor | Evidence that you understand the problem and the method | Months of redirection later |
| Departmental postgraduate committee | A feasible plan with a clear question | Proposal returned for revision |
| External examiner (eventually) | A thesis whose scope matches its claims | A hard viva |
| You, in 18 months | A record of what you promised and why | Scope creep and lost direction |

Write for the committee. Your supervisor already knows the context; the committee does not.

## Mindset

- **Specific beats ambitious.** "Develop a quantum-ready differentiable LBM solver for all multiphase flows" is a career. "Quantify the accuracy and cost trade-off of a differentiable D2Q9 LBM solver against a finite-volume baseline on three canonical two-phase benchmarks" is a thesis.
- **Honest beats impressive.** Name the limitations before the committee does.
- **Done beats perfect.** Get a complete draft to your supervisor early. Iterate.
