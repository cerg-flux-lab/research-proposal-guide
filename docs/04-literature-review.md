# 04. Literature review

## Purpose

The literature review in a proposal has one job: to make the gap inevitable. By the time the reader reaches the problem statement, they should already be expecting it. A review that summarises papers one after another ("Smith did X. Jones did Y.") does not do this. A review that *argues* does.

## Structure: funnel, not list

```
Field               ->  Sub-field          ->  Specific problem     ->  Gap
(2 or 3 paragraphs)     (1 to 2 pages)          (1 to 2 pages)           (1 paragraph)
Establish why the       What approaches         What has been tried,     What remains,
field matters and       exist; their            what worked, what        stated precisely
what the open           strengths and           did not, and why
questions are           limits
```

Organise by **theme or method**, never by author or by date. Each subsection should end with a sentence of the form "However, ..." or "What remains unclear is ...". Those sentences, collected, are your gap.

## What to read, and how much

- **Seminal works** that defined the method (the LBM review by Chen and Doolen, the original PINN paper by Raissi et al., the relevant V&V standard). Read these in full.
- **The five to ten papers closest to your question.** Read these in full, more than once. Reproduce a figure from one of them if you can; it is the fastest way to find out whether you understand it.
- **Recent reviews** (last three years) to map the landscape and find the papers above.
- **Everything else**: abstract, figures, conclusions. Cite only if it carries weight in your argument.

A proposal review in engineering typically cites 40 to 80 sources for a PhD and 25 to 50 for an MEng. The number matters far less than whether each citation does work.

## Critical reading

For each key paper, record in your notes:

1. What question did they ask?
2. What method, at what fidelity, on what cases?
3. What did they find, with what uncertainty?
4. What did they *not* do, and did they say why?
5. How does this bear on my gap?

Item 5 is the sentence that goes in your proposal. Items 1 to 4 are the evidence you need for the viva.

## Managing references

- Use a reference manager from day one (the UP Library supports EndNote; Zotero and JabRef are free and work well with BibTeX).
- Keep one `.bib` file for the whole degree. Your proposal, papers and thesis all draw from it.
- Never cite a paper you have not opened. Never cite from a secondary source without reading the primary. If an AI tool suggests a reference, treat it as a search hint, not a citation, until you have the PDF in front of you.
- Use the UP Library's systematic review guidance if your topic warrants a structured search; record your search strings and databases either way, so the review is reproducible.

## Tables that earn their place

A comparison table of prior studies (method, case, Reynolds number range, validation data, reported error) is often the single most persuasive element of a proposal review. It makes the gap visible as an empty cell.
